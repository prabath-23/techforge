## 1. URL shortener (like bit.ly)

### What it must do

- Turn a long URL into a short one like `sho.rt/aZ3k9Qx` (optionally with a custom name or expiry date).
- Visiting the short link quickly redirects to the original URL, in under ~50 ms for almost everyone.
- Count clicks for the link's owner.

### The numbers

About 100 million new links a month (~40 per second), read 100 times more often (~4,000 redirects per second, ~10,000 at peak), stored for 10 years: about 12 billion links and **6 TB** of data. Seven characters from a 62-character alphabet give 3.5 trillion possible codes, plenty.

### The hard parts

**Generating unique codes without a bottleneck.** Three approaches:

- **Hash the URL** and take the first 7 characters: the same URL always gets the same code, but two different URLs can collide, and you must check.
- **A counter** converted to base 62: unique by construction. One central counter would be a bottleneck and single point of failure, so a **key range service** hands each server a block of a million numbers; each server then counts locally. (Sequential codes are guessable; if that matters, scramble the number with a reversible mixing function first.)
- **Pre-generate random codes** offline into a pool, and let servers claim batches.

**301 or 302?** A 301 ("moved permanently") is cached by browsers, so repeat clicks never reach you, less load, but uncountable. A 302 sends every click through you, which is what analytics needs.

**Reads dominate.** With 100 reads per write, the design is all about the read path: CDN, then Redis, then the database. The write path is comparatively quiet.

**Extras:** rate-limit link creation to stop spammers, scan for phishing links, and delete expired links lazily (when they're next requested) plus a background cleanup job.

## 2. News feed (like Instagram or X's home timeline)

### What it must do

- Users post (text, photos). Users follow other users.
- Each user sees a **home feed** of recent posts from the people they follow, ranked.
- 500 million daily users; loading the feed must take under ~200 ms. A post may take a few seconds to appear in followers' feeds.

### The numbers

Hundreds of thousands of feed loads per second, but "only" thousands of new posts per second. Each feed merges posts from hundreds of followed accounts. **Building each feed from scratch on every load is far too slow** so feeds must be prepared ahead of time.

### The hard part: when to build each feed

> [!ANALOGY] **Fan-out on write** is like a newspaper delivering a copy to every subscriber's doorstep the moment it's printed: lots of work up front, but reading is instant. **Fan-out on read** is like everyone walking to the newsstand and assembling their own paper from many publishers: no upfront work, but every reader waits.

- **Fan-out on write (push):** when you post, insert the post into every follower's timeline. Reads are a single fast lookup. But when a celebrity with 100 million followers posts, that's 100 million writes for one post.
- **Fan-out on read (pull):** when you open the app, fetch recent posts from everyone you follow and merge them. No write amplification, but every feed load is slow and expensive.
- **Hybrid, what large networks actually do:** push for normal accounts; for accounts above a follower threshold, **don't** fan out, merge their latest posts in when each follower opens the feed.

**Other details:** don't fan out to accounts inactive for 30+ days (rebuild their timeline if they return); handle deleted posts by filtering them out when the feed is loaded; and use **cursor-based pagination** for infinite scroll, so new posts arriving at the top don't cause duplicates.

## 3. Chat and messaging (like WhatsApp or Slack)

### What it must do

- One-to-one and group messages, delivered in real time when the recipient is online.
- Delivered even if the recipient is offline (as soon as they come back), in the **right order** never duplicated.
- "Sent", "delivered" and "read" receipts; online/last-seen status; history synced across devices.

### The hard parts

- **Millions of open connections.** Each gateway server holds a huge number of mostly idle WebSocket connections using event loops (OS track, chapter 10). A registry maps each user's devices to gateways.
- **Order and no duplicates.** Each conversation gets its own increasing **sequence number**; clients display messages by that number and ask for anything missing after reconnecting. A client-generated message ID makes retries harmless (idempotency).
- **Storage.** Message volume is huge and mostly recent messages are read, in order, a perfect fit for wide-column, LSM-based databases partitioned by conversation (chapter 3). Discord stores trillions of messages this way.
- **Groups.** For small groups, deliver to each member. For huge channels (Slack or Discord servers with 100,000 members), store each message once per channel and let members read the channel, only sending notifications individually.
- **Presence is surprisingly expensive** every online/offline change could notify hundreds of contacts. Send updates only to contacts who are currently online, and batch them.
- **End-to-end encryption** (WhatsApp, Signal): the server only routes encrypted blobs it can't read. Keys belong to each device, which makes multi-device history sync much harder.

### The hard parts

- **Adaptive bitrate streaming** (HLS, DASH): videos are stored as short segments at several qualities. The player picks the quality **segment by segment** based on its measured download speed and how much video it has buffered. That's why quality sometimes drops briefly instead of the video stopping to buffer.
- **Delivery cost dominates.** Almost all traffic is served from CDNs. **Netflix's Open Connect** places servers inside internet providers' networks and preloads them overnight with what that area is likely to watch tomorrow.
- **Storage cost.** Many renditions of every video add up. Platforms encode popular videos with more efficient (but slower to encode) formats like AV1, and keep rarely watched videos in cheaper, slower storage.
- **View counts** are counted approximately and asynchronously through event streams, the famous "301 views" freeze on YouTube's early days was a pause while views were verified as genuine.

### What it must do

### The design

### How it fits together

- **Partitioning:** consistent hashing with many virtual nodes per machine (chapter 2) spreads keys evenly and moves little data when machines join or leave.
- **Replication:** each key is stored on the next N (say 3) distinct machines around the ring, placed in different availability zones.
- **Tunable consistency:** per request, choose W and R. W = 1 gives the fastest writes; R + W > N gives reads that see the latest write (chapter 4).
- **Staying available during failures:** if a replica is down, another node temporarily accepts its writes (**hinted handoff**) and passes them on later.
- **Converging:** **read repair** fixes stale copies when they're read; **anti-entropy** with **Merkle trees** (trees of hashes that let two replicas find their differences by comparing just a few hashes) fixes everything else in the background.
- **Conflicts:** concurrent writes to the same key are detected with **version vectors**; resolve them with "last write wins" (simple, may lose data) or by merging (Amazon's original shopping cart merged items from both versions, which is why deleted items occasionally came back).
- **Membership:** **gossip** spreads who's alive, with no central coordinator to fail.

### The hard parts

- **Politeness:** one website's queue is handled by one worker at a time, with delays between requests and respect for `robots.txt`. Crawling a small business's site too aggressively is effectively a denial-of-service attack.
- **Not crawling forever:** normalize URLs, remember every URL seen (a **Bloom filter** saves enormous memory, DSA track, chapter 3), and limit depth to avoid **crawler traps** such as endless calendar pages ("next month" forever).
- **Duplicate content:** many URLs show the same page. **SimHash/MinHash** fingerprints spot near-duplicates even when small details differ.
- **Freshness:** news sites change every minute; most pages rarely change. Recrawl each page at a rate matching how often it has changed in the past.
- **Scale:** everything is partitioned by website (hash of the hostname), so each machine owns a set of websites end to end.

### The hard parts

- **"Who's near me?"** A normal database index can't efficiently answer "everything within 2 km". - **Location updates are a firehose** of short-lived data: keep them in memory with expiry, not in the main database.
- **Never double-book a driver:** accepting an offer must atomically change the driver from "available" to "on trip" (a conditional update or lock, chapter 3).
- **Surge pricing:** stream processing (chapter 6) compares demand and supply per area over the last few minutes.

### The hard parts

- **Speed:** precompute the top-k completions **at every trie node** so a request needs no searching at all, just walking down the typed letters (DSA track, chapter 5).
