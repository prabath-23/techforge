## 1. Ride-hailing (like Uber or Ola)

> [!STORY] You tap "Book" outside a railway station at 9 pm. Within seconds the app finds a driver 600 m away, shows the car moving towards you on the map, quotes a price that's a little higher because it's raining, and later charges your card exactly once. Behind that tap: millions of drivers each sending their GPS position every few seconds, a matching system that must never give one driver two riders, and pricing that reacts to supply and demand street by street.

### What it must do

- Riders request a trip; nearby available drivers are offered it; one accepts; both see each other live on the map; trip, fare and payment are recorded.
- **Scale:** ~5 million active drivers sending a location every 4 seconds → **~1.25 million location updates per second** at peak. Matching must take a few seconds; location on the rider's map should lag by only a few seconds.
- **Priorities:** availability and low latency for location and matching; strong correctness for trip state and payments (no double assignment, no double charge).

### The hard parts

- **Location write volume:** ~1.25M updates per second, but each only replaces the previous position. Keep it **in memory** sharded by geography, and write a sampled history to a log for analytics, never one database row update per GPS ping.
- **Finding nearby drivers:** a spatial index (H3 hexagons, geohash or quadtree, Algorithms track, chapter 11). Query the cell and neighbouring rings, then rank by **road ETA** not straight-line distance.
- **No double assignment:** two riders' matchers may pick the same driver. The assignment is a **conditional update** (`SET status='on_trip' WHERE driver_id=D AND status='available'`) or a short lock on the driver (Databases track, chapter 6). The loser retries with the next candidate.
- **Surge pricing:** aggregate open requests and available drivers per cell every minute (stream processing over Kafka), smooth the multiplier, and cap it.
- **Region partitioning:** cities are largely independent, so services shard by city/region, limiting blast radius and keeping data close to users.
- **Unreliable phones:** apps go through tunnels and lose connection; trip state lives on the server, every command is idempotent, and apps resync on reconnect.

## 2. Video platform (like YouTube)

> [!STORY] A creator uploads a 2 GB, 20-minute 4K video from a laptop on shaky hotel Wi-Fi. An hour later, viewers in Chennai on 4G, in São Paulo on fibre and on a smart TV in London are all watching it smoothly, each at a different quality, with the first frame appearing in under a second. More than 500 hours of video are uploaded to YouTube every minute, and over a billion hours are watched every day.

### What it must do

- Upload videos (resumable), process them into streamable formats, play them on any device and network, with view counts, recommendations and comments.
- **Scale:** uploads ~500 hours/minute; reads hugely dominate writes (views ≫ uploads). Storage in exabytes; egress bandwidth is the largest cost.
- **Priorities:** fast start and no buffering for viewers; durability of uploads; cost efficiency.

### The hard parts

- **Transcoding cost and speed:** split into chunks and transcode in parallel; prioritize popular creators; encode expensive codecs (AV1) only for videos that get many views, since better compression saves bandwidth only when watched often.
- **Adaptive bitrate streaming (HLS/DASH):** small segments at several qualities let the player switch every few seconds, the key to "no buffering" on variable networks.
- **CDN economics:** the long tail. A small fraction of videos get most views and live at the edge; rarely watched videos are served from regional caches or origin. YouTube and Netflix place cache servers **inside ISPs**.
- **View counts:** billions of increments per day, batch and aggregate in a stream processor, deduplicate and filter fraud; counts can be slightly delayed.
- **Resumable uploads** and pre-signed URLs keep huge files off API servers.

## 3. Messaging (like WhatsApp)

> [!STORY] In 2014, when Facebook bought WhatsApp for $19 billion, the service had around 450 million monthly users, and about 32 engineers. Its servers, built on Erlang, could each hold about **two million** simultaneous connections. Today WhatsApp handles over 100 billion messages a day, all end-to-end encrypted, so WhatsApp's own servers can't read them. The design is a lesson in doing a few things extremely well.

### What it must do

- One-to-one and group messages, delivery and read receipts (✓ sent, ✓✓ delivered, blue ✓✓ read), online/last seen, media sharing, works on flaky mobile networks, **end-to-end encryption**.
- **Scale:** 100B+ messages/day → ~1.2M messages/second on average, several times that at peaks (New Year's midnight). Hundreds of millions of concurrent connections.
- **Priorities:** never lose a message, deliver in order per chat, low latency, minimal server-side storage.

### The hard parts

- **Millions of connections per server:** event-driven servers (Erlang processes, epoll, Networking track, chapter 10), tuned kernels and heartbeats.
- **Exactly-once-ish delivery:** client-generated **message IDs** make retries idempotent; acknowledgments at each hop; per-chat **sequence numbers** keep order; the receiver deduplicates.
- **Store-and-forward, not store-forever:** the server keeps messages only until delivered, less storage, less to leak.
- **End-to-end encryption (Signal protocol):** keys are created on devices; the server distributes only **public** keys; each message uses a fresh key (forward secrecy). Consequences for design: no server-side search of message content, encrypted backups, and **multi-device** support means encrypting a copy for each of the recipient's devices.
- **Groups:** fan-out on write to each member (capped group sizes keep it manageable).
- **Presence ("online", "last seen")** is high-volume and low-value: update lazily, only to contacts who are looking, and allow it to be slightly stale.

## 4. Photo sharing and feed (like Instagram)

> [!STORY] Instagram reached 30 million users with a tiny team in its first years, running on PostgreSQL, Redis and Django. When a celebrity with 600 million followers posts a photo, the system must make it appear in feeds without trying to write it into 600 million feeds at once, while regular users' posts appear for their few hundred followers instantly.

### What it must do

- Upload photos/videos with captions; follow users; a **home feed** of posts from people you follow (ranked); likes, comments; profile pages.
- **Scale:** ~2 billion monthly users; hundreds of millions of posts per day; feed reads vastly outnumber posts (perhaps 100:1).
- **Priorities:** fast feed loads (< 200 ms), high availability; feeds can be slightly stale (eventual consistency is fine).

### The hard parts

- **Fan-out on write vs read** (the hybrid): push to followers' feeds for most users (fast reads), pull at read time for celebrity accounts (avoids writing to hundreds of millions of feeds). This is the classic news-feed trade-off from chapter 8's Twitter design.
- **Ranking:** gather a few thousand candidates, score with ML models, mix in diversity rules (don't show five posts in a row from one person).
- **Media:** resize at upload, serve via CDN, store originals cheaply; the database holds only metadata.
- **IDs:** time-sortable 64-bit IDs (Instagram's own scheme combined a timestamp, a shard ID and a sequence) so posts sort by time without lookups.
- **Counters (likes):** sharded counters and asynchronous aggregation for hot posts; exact counts can lag slightly.

## 5. Maps and navigation (like Google Maps)

> [!STORY] You ask for directions from Bengaluru airport to Koramangala at 6 pm. In under a second, Maps returns three routes with ETAs that account for current traffic, including a jam that formed eight minutes ago on the outer ring road, and redraws the map smoothly as you zoom out to see the whole city. Road networks with hundreds of millions of road segments, live speed data from millions of phones, and map imagery for the whole planet all come together in that one request.

### What it must do

- Show a zoomable map anywhere on Earth; search places; compute routes (car, bike, walk, transit) with traffic-aware ETAs; turn-by-turn navigation with rerouting.
- **Scale:** over a billion users; tens of thousands of route requests per second; the global road graph has hundreds of millions of nodes and edges.
- **Priorities:** fast map rendering and routing (< 1 s), accurate ETAs, fresh traffic data.

### The hard parts

- **Fast routing on a huge graph:** plain Dijkstra (Algorithms track) on a continent explores millions of nodes. **Contraction hierarchies** precompute shortcut edges so queries explore only thousands, in milliseconds. Live traffic changes weights, so systems use variants (customizable route planning) that can update weights quickly without full re-preprocessing.
- **Map tiles:** the world at ~20 zoom levels is billions of tiles; pre-generate popular ones, generate rare ones on demand, cache everything at the CDN. **Vector tiles** let the app restyle and rotate maps smoothly.
- **Traffic and ETA:** map-matching noisy GPS to roads; combining live data with historical patterns (and, at Google, graph neural networks) to predict travel time for the time you'll actually reach each segment.
- **Geospatial search:** spatial indexes (S2 cells, R-trees) for "near me" and geocoding.
- **Offline maps:** downloadable regions of tiles and a compact routing graph for when there's no signal.

> [!RECAP]
> - **Ride-hailing:** in-memory geo index for 1M+ location updates/s, matching by road ETA, conditional updates to prevent double assignment, surge per cell, city sharding.
> - **Video:** resumable uploads, parallel chunked transcoding into adaptive-bitrate segments, CDN-heavy delivery, aggregated view counts.
> - **Messaging:** persistent connections, session registry routing, store-and-forward with idempotent message IDs and acks, Signal-protocol end-to-end encryption.
> - **Photo feed:** media via CDN, hybrid fan-out (push for most, pull for celebrities), ML ranking, time-sortable IDs.
> - **Maps:** CDN-cached vector tiles, contraction-hierarchy routing over a partitioned graph, a streaming traffic pipeline updating edge weights.
